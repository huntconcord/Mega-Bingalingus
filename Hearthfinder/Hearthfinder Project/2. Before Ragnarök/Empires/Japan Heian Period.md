## Background

Emperor Kanmu moved the capital to Heian-kyō, modern Kyoto, in 794. The new capital was designed according to Chinese-influenced urban principles.

The imperial court gradually reduced its direct involvement in provincial government. Powerful aristocratic families, especially the Fujiwara, increased their influence through court offices and marriages with the imperial family.

By 900, Emperor Daigo occupied the throne, but aristocratic families and major religious institutions were becoming increasingly powerful.

## Culture

Early Heian culture retained strong Chinese influences in government, architecture, religion, literature, and writing.

Buddhist schools became increasingly important. Saichō established the Tendai tradition at Mount Hiei, while Kūkai established Shingon Buddhism at Mount Kōya.

Court culture emphasized:

- Poetry
- Calligraphy
- Music
- Formal clothing
- Rank
- Ceremony
- Knowledge of Chinese literature

Kana writing was developing from simplified Chinese characters. It eventually allowed Japanese authors to write more freely in their spoken language.

The exceptionally refined court culture commonly associated with works such as _The Tale of Genji_ developed more fully after 900.

## Military

### Organization

Japan’s Chinese-inspired mass conscription system declined during the late eighth century. After 792, the court increasingly relied on:

- Imperial guards
- Provincial officials
- Local military families
- Mounted warriors
- Professional retainers
- Regional militias
- Specialized police and pursuit forces

These warriors were predecessors of the samurai, but the fully developed samurai government and warrior code of later centuries did not yet exist.

### Gear and equipment

Mounted warriors commonly used:

- Long asymmetrical bows
- Arrows with specialized heads
- Curved or increasingly curved swords
- Spears
- Daggers

Armor developed from earlier lamellar traditions. Elite warriors wore armor made from small iron or leather plates bound together with cords.

Infantry used:

- Bows
- Spears
- Swords
- Wooden shields
- Clubs or other simple weapons

The Japanese bow was already extremely long, reaching roughly 2.5 meters in the Heian period.

### Tactics and fighting style

Mounted archery was increasingly important among provincial elites.

Common tactics included:

- Mounted archery
- Massed infantry archery
- Raids against settlements
- Pursuit of rebels and bandits
- Ambushes in forests and mountain passes
- Burning enemy buildings
- Attacking isolated groups
- Using local guides and provincial intelligence

Warriors could fire from horseback before closing with swords or spears. Large infantry formations still mattered, especially during campaigns against the Emishi in northern Honshu.

Combat was not yet dominated by formal sword duels between individual samurai. Bows, surprise, numbers, and attacks against homes or supplies were generally more practical.

### Fortifications and siege warfare

Fortifications usually consisted of:

- Wooden stockades
- Ditches
- Earthen walls
- Defended residences
- Mountain positions
- Government compounds

Attackers relied on fire, ladders, missiles, direct assault, and blockade. Massive stone castles belonged to a much later period.

### Military quirks

Warriors were often provincial officials, land managers, policemen, or tax enforcers as well as soldiers.

The court attempted to control violence by granting particular families authority to pursue rebels. This helped military families develop private networks and experience that eventually made them more powerful than the court itself.

## Politics

The emperor remained the formal source of political authority. Government offices followed institutions originally modeled on Tang China.

In practice, aristocratic families competed for court rank and access to the imperial household. The Fujiwara expanded their power by marrying daughters to emperors and becoming maternal relatives of future rulers.

Private estates called _shōen_ increasingly escaped direct provincial taxation. Aristocrats, temples, and local landholders benefited from these estates.

By 900, Japan remained under imperial government. The age of shoguns had not begun, but the foundations of a provincial warrior class were appearing.
