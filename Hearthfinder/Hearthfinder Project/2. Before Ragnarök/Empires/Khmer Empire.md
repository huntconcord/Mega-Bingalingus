## Background

The Khmer Empire traditionally begins in 802, when Jayavarman II was consecrated as a universal ruler on Mount Mahendraparvata. He attempted to unite the formerly divided Khmer territories and free them from foreign influence, although historians debate how much territory he directly controlled.

Jayavarman II and his successors established several capitals around the northern shore of Tonlé Sap. Indravarman I, ruling from 877 to 889, constructed temples and one of the earliest large reservoirs associated with Angkorian hydraulic engineering.

Yasovarman I became king in 889 and established Yasodharapura near Phnom Bakheng. This settlement became the core of Angkor, the empire’s future capital.

## Culture

Khmer culture combined indigenous traditions with religious and political ideas derived from India.

Hinduism was especially important to the monarchy. Khmer rulers principally honored Shiva and Vishnu, although Buddhism and local religious traditions remained present.

Temples served several purposes:

- Worship centers
- Symbols of royal authority
- Landholding institutions
- Administrative centers
- Memorials to rulers and their ancestors

Sanskrit was used for formal religious and royal inscriptions, while Old Khmer was used for administrative information and local records.

The king’s authority was reinforced through ceremonies connecting him to divine protection and universal kingship. However, the common description of every Khmer ruler as a literal “god-king” oversimplifies a more complicated relationship between kingship, temples, ancestors, and Hindu deities.

## Military

### Organization

The early Khmer Empire probably lacked a continuously mobilized standing army. The king raised forces through:

- Royal household warriors
- Regional governors
- Local chiefs
- Village levies
- Noble retainers
- Allied or subordinate rulers
- Elephant handlers and specialized troops

Local leaders commanded their own populations and owed military service to the king. Conquered chiefs could retain authority after swearing loyalty and providing soldiers, labor, and tribute.

### Gear and equipment

Early Khmer soldiers probably used:

- Spears
- Javelins
- Bows
- Straight swords
- Long knives
- Axes or chopping weapons
- Round or rectangular shields

Most infantry likely wore little metal armor. Protection probably consisted of shields, layered cloth, leather, and possibly helmets made from organic materials.

Elite warriors and commanders may have possessed metal helmets, body armor, and higher-quality swords. Elephants carried rulers, commanders, archers, or spearmen and acted as mobile command platforms.

Later Angkorian reliefs show extensive military equipment, but these carvings were produced centuries after 900 and cannot automatically be treated as perfect depictions of the earliest Khmer armies.

### Tactics and fighting style

Khmer armies depended primarily on large infantry forces supported by elephants and smaller numbers of cavalry.

Likely tactics included:

- Dense spear formations
- Archery before close combat
- Elephant attacks against unsettled formations
- Ambushes in forests
- Attacks along rivers and causeways
- Destruction of crops and settlements
- Capturing regional centers to force submission
- Using rivers to transport troops and supplies

Elephants were valuable but vulnerable. They could frighten infantry and provide commanders with a clear view, but missiles, difficult terrain, or panic could make them dangerous to their own army.

### Siege and waterborne warfare

Khmer warfare frequently centered on fortified settlements rather than massive stone castles. Attackers could use:

- Fire
- Ladders
- Blockades
- Earthworks
- Battering equipment
- Cutting food or water supplies

Boats were essential for operating around Tonlé Sap and along Cambodia’s rivers. They transported infantry, supplies, tribute, and royal officials.

### Military quirks

Khmer military power was closely connected to control over people and agricultural land. Capturing a region could mean relocating its population and incorporating its leaders rather than occupying every village with soldiers.

Royal construction also supported warfare. Reservoirs, roads, temples, and settlements helped concentrate food, labor, and administrative power near the capital.

## Politics

The Khmer king ruled through a network of relatives, priests, officials, temple authorities, and regional chiefs.

Political power depended on:

- Personal loyalty to the king
- Control of agricultural labor
- Temple patronage
- Marriage alliances
- Military victories
- Distribution of land and offices
- Recognition by regional elites

The empire was not yet a uniformly administered territorial state. Royal authority was strongest near major capitals and depended on cooperation from local leaders farther away.

By 900, Yasovarman I was consolidating power around the newly established capital of Yasodharapura. The monumental Angkorian civilization existed, but its greatest territorial expansion and most famous temples still lay in the future.
