## Background

East Francia was created through the Treaty of Verdun in 843 and given to Louis the German. It contained most of the Frankish territories east of the Rhine.

Its population included several major Germanic-speaking groups, including:

- Saxons
- Bavarians
- Franks
- Thuringians
- Alemanni or Swabians

East Francia fought against Great Moravia, Slavic communities, Danes, and other peoples along its northern and eastern frontiers. Near the end of the 800s, the kingdom also began facing attacks from the Magyars.

East Francia eventually contributed to the formation of medieval Germany and the Holy Roman Empire.

## Culture

East Frankish culture combined Germanic regional traditions with Carolingian government and Latin Christianity.

Latin was used by the church and government, while the population spoke several Germanic dialects. These dialects contributed to the later development of the German language.

Monasteries served as centers of:

- Education
- Manuscript production
- Religious instruction
- Agricultural organization
- Royal administration
- Missionary activity

East Frankish missionaries attempted to convert Slavic communities beyond the kingdom’s borders. This sometimes created competition with Byzantine missionaries working in the same regions.

Regional identities remained strong. A Saxon, Bavarian, or Swabian noble might identify more closely with his own region than with East Francia as a unified kingdom.

## Military

### Organization

The East Frankish army included:

- The king’s personal troops
- Regional noble retinues
- Mounted warriors
- Infantry levies
- Forces raised by bishops and abbots
- Frontier troops
- Allied Slavic forces

Regional nobles played an especially important role because East Francia possessed several long and unstable frontiers.

### Gear and equipment

East Frankish mounted warriors carried:

- Spears or lances
- Straight swords
- Round shields
- Javelins
- Daggers

Elite warriors wore mail and iron helmets. Ordinary infantry used spears, axes, bows, javelins, wooden shields, and large knives.

The military equipment remained broadly similar to that of the former unified Frankish Empire.

### Tactics and fighting style

East Frankish warfare emphasized frontier campaigns against Slavic states and mobile enemies.

Common tactics included:

- Mounted raids
- Infantry shield formations
- Attacks on enemy strongholds
- Burning settlements and crops
- Establishing border fortifications
- Taking hostages from defeated leaders
- Supporting rival factions within enemy states
- Forcing neighboring rulers to pay tribute

East Frankish kings frequently combined military action with diplomacy. They supported rival Slavic princes, arranged alliances, and encouraged internal rebellions within enemy territories.

Against Great Moravia, East Frankish armies conducted repeated invasions but struggled to maintain permanent control.

### Fighting the Magyars

The Magyars began entering the Carpathian Basin near the end of the 800s. Their military depended heavily on fast cavalry and mounted archery.

East Frankish armies were poorly prepared for Magyar tactics, which included:

- Rapid raids
- Mounted archery
- Feigned retreats
- Attacks on isolated forces
- Avoidance of heavily defended positions

Traditional Frankish armies often moved too slowly to catch Magyar horsemen. This became a much greater problem during the 900s.

### Siege and defensive warfare

East Frankish armies attacked wooden and earthwork fortifications using fire, ladders, blockades, and basic siege machines.

Frontier defenses included:

- Fortified towns
- Royal strongholds
- Monasteries
- Earthworks
- Border outposts
- River crossings

### Military quirks

East Frankish kings regularly used one neighboring people against another. A ruler might recruit Slavic allies for one campaign and fight those same groups several years later.

Armies were strongly regional. Bavarian forces were especially important along the Danube, while Saxon armies concentrated on the northern and northeastern frontiers.

## Politics

East Francia was ruled by the Carolingian dynasty, but the king depended heavily on powerful regional nobles and church leaders.

Louis the German attempted to maintain unity through military victories, family alliances, and cooperation with the regional aristocracy. After his death, his territories were divided among his sons.

Charles the Fat briefly reunited much of the former Frankish Empire, but he was removed in 887 after failing to respond effectively to Viking attacks and political opposition.

Arnulf of Carinthia became king of East Francia and defeated a Viking army at the Battle of the Dyle in 891. He also intervened in Great Moravia and Italy.

By 900, East Francia was increasingly divided into powerful regional duchies. The king remained important, but Saxon, Bavarian, Franconian, and Swabian nobles exercised substantial authority within their territories.
