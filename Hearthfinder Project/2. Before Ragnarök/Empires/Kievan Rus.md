## Background

Kievan Rus’ began developing during the 800s along the river systems connecting the Baltic Sea, the Black Sea, and the Byzantine Empire.

The region was inhabited by numerous groups, including:

- Eastern Slavic tribes
- Finnic peoples
- Baltic peoples
- Norse traders and warriors called Varangians
- Steppe peoples connected to the Khazar Khaganate

Norse merchants and warriors traveled south from Scandinavia in search of silver, furs, wax, honey, enslaved people, and access to Byzantine markets.

According to the much later _Primary Chronicle_, a Varangian leader named Rurik took power around Novgorod in 862. His relative or successor Oleg captured Kyiv in 882 and made it the center of his growing state.

These dates and stories are partly legendary and were recorded centuries later. Nevertheless, archaeological evidence confirms extensive Scandinavian trade and settlement in the region.

Kyiv became important because it controlled movement along the Dnieper River. Its location connected northern trading settlements with the Black Sea and Constantinople.

## Culture

![[Rus Clothes.png|484]]

The population of Kievan Rus’ was primarily Slavic, but its early ruling and military elite included many people of Scandinavian origin. Over time, the Varangians adopted local languages and customs and became part of a developing Rus’ identity.

Early Rus’ society followed several pagan traditions. Important gods included:

- Perun, associated with thunder and warfare
- Veles, associated with livestock, wealth, and the underworld
- Dazhbog, associated with the sun and prosperity
- Mokosh, associated with fertility and household life

Religious practices included sacrifices, wooden idols, sacred groves, funeral feasts, and burial mounds. Wealthy warriors were sometimes buried with weapons, jewelry, horses, boats, or other possessions.

Kievan Rus’ had contact with Christianity, Islam, Judaism, and other religions through merchants and neighboring states. However, the Rus’ did not officially adopt Christianity until Vladimir the Great’s conversion in 988.

The culture was strongly connected to trade. Settlements such as Kyiv and Novgorod contained Slavic, Norse, Finnic, and foreign merchants.

## Military

### Organization

![[Rus Horsemen.png]]

The central military unit was the prince’s personal retinue, called the _druzhina_. Its members served as warriors, advisers, bodyguards, and tax collectors.

A Rus’ army could include:

- The prince’s professional retinue
- Varangian warriors
- Slavic tribal levies
- Local fighting forces
- Archers and hunters
- Allied warriors from neighboring peoples

The druzhina received weapons, food, gifts, and shares of tribute or captured wealth from the prince. Its loyalty was personal, meaning warriors could abandon a prince who failed to reward them.

### Gear and equipment

![[Rus 10th Cent Gear.png]]

Wealthy Rus’ and Varangian warriors commonly carried:

- Spears
- Straight, double-edged swords
- One-handed axes
- Large fighting axes
- Bows
- Seaxes or fighting knives
- Round wooden shields

Elite warriors might wear mail shirts and conical iron helmets. Many swords were imported from or inspired by weapons made in the Frankish territories.

Ordinary Slavic troops were more likely to carry:

- Spears
- Bows
- Axes
- Javelins
- Knives
- Wooden shields

Metal armor was expensive, so most warriors depended on shields, thick clothing, and mobility for protection.

### Tactics and fighting style
![[Rus 9th Cent Military.png|700]]
Early Rus’ armies generally fought on foot after traveling to the battlefield by boat. Warriors could form shield lines, with spearmen in front and archers or javelin throwers behind them.

Common tactics included:

- Sudden riverborne attacks
- Coastal and riverside raids
- Shield-wall fighting
- Ambushes in forests
- Attacks on isolated settlements
- Rapid withdrawal before enemy reinforcements arrived
- Controlling river crossings and portage routes

The Rus’ possessed less cavalry than steppe peoples such as the Khazars. Horses were used for transportation and scouting, but boats remained the most important form of strategic movement.

As the Rus’ expanded southward, they gradually adopted more mounted tactics from their steppe neighbors.

### Ships and river warfare

![[Rus BOAT.png|658]]

Rus’ vessels could travel through shallow rivers and across open water. Some were based on Scandinavian shipbuilding traditions, while others were large dugout boats modified with additional planks and sails.

Rus’ warriors could:

- Row or sail along rivers
- Carry ships around waterfalls and rapids
- Drag vessels between different river systems
- Launch surprise attacks from the water
- Transport trade goods and soldiers in the same vessels

The ability to move boats overland allowed the Rus’ to connect rivers flowing toward the Baltic, Black, and Caspian seas.

### Siege warfare

![[RU Fort.png|598]]

Before 900, the Rus’ had relatively limited siege equipment. They were more effective at raiding, intimidating settlements, and demanding tribute than capturing major stone fortifications.

Against fortified towns, they could use:

- Ladders
- Fire
- Blockades
- Basic battering equipment
- Surprise attacks
- Negotiated surrender

More sophisticated siege techniques developed later through contact with the Byzantine Empire and other neighboring states.

### Military quirks

Rus’ warriors were often both merchants and raiders. The same group might trade peacefully in one city, collect tribute in another, and attack a third.

Their ships were not simply naval vessels. They functioned as transportation, trading platforms, troop carriers, and mobile camps. This made the Rus’ unusually mobile across the enormous river networks of Eastern Europe.

## Politics

![[RU Court.png]]

Before the late 800s, the region was not a unified kingdom. It consisted of tribal communities, trading settlements, and territories that paid tribute to stronger neighbors such as the Khazars.

Rus’ princes ruled with the support of their druzhina. Their authority depended on their ability to:

- Protect trade routes
- Collect tribute
- Reward warriors
- Defeat rival rulers
- Control important towns and rivers
- Negotiate with foreign powers

Oleg’s capture of Kyiv around 882 traditionally marks the beginning of Kievan Rus’ as a political state. From Kyiv, he extended authority over nearby Slavic communities and challenged Khazar influence.

By 900, Kievan Rus’ was still a loose network rather than a centralized empire. It had no extensive bureaucracy or standardized provincial government. Its power rested on Kyiv, river trade, tribute, military retainers, and agreements with local tribal leaders.
