## Background

The Pala dynasty arose in Bengal during the eighth century. According to later tradition, regional leaders elected Gopala to restore order after a period of conflict.

Dharmapala and Devapala expanded Pala influence across Bengal, Bihar, and portions of northern India. They competed with the Gurjara-Pratiharas and Rashtrakutas for influence over Kannauj and the northern Indian plains.

Around 900, the Palas were ruled by Narayanapala or one of his immediate successors. The empire was experiencing a period of reduced power but still controlled important territory in eastern India.

## Culture

The Palas were major supporters of Mahayana and Vajrayana Buddhism. They also patronized Hindu institutions and ruled a population containing Buddhists, Hindus, and followers of local traditions.

Pala rulers supported major monasteries and educational centers, including:

- Nalanda
- Vikramashila
- Somapura
- Odantapuri

These institutions attracted scholars and monks from across Asia.

Pala art became famous for:

- Bronze Buddhist sculpture
- Stone carving
- Illuminated manuscripts
- Temple and monastery architecture

Bengal’s river systems and access to the Bay of Bengal connected the empire to trade networks extending toward Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia.

## Military

### Organization

The Pala army included:

- Royal household troops
- Infantry
- Archers
- Cavalry
- War elephants
- River and coastal vessels
- Forces supplied by subordinate rulers
- Retinues maintained by regional landholders

The Palas depended substantially on _samantas_, subordinate rulers or nobles who provided tribute and military contingents.

This allowed the emperor to assemble a large army but created problems when powerful _samantas_ withheld troops or rebelled.

### Gear and equipment

Pala infantry used:

- Bows
- Spears
- Javelins
- Straight or curved swords
- Axes
- Maces
- Daggers
- Shields

Elite soldiers wore mail, scale armor, helmets, or padded protection. Ordinary troops carried lighter equipment.

Cavalry used bows, lances, swords, and shields. Quality horses were often imported from northwestern India, Central Asia, or maritime traders.

War elephants carried drivers and warriors armed with bows, javelins, or spears. Elephants were employed throughout ancient and medieval Indian warfare and remained a central symbol of royal military power.

### Tactics and fighting style

Pala armies used combined-arms formations.

Typical tactics included:

- Archers weakening the enemy
- Infantry holding the main line
- Elephants attacking dense formations
- Cavalry protecting the flanks
- Keeping reserves behind the elephants
- Raiding enemy farmland
- Capturing fortified cities
- Forcing subordinate rulers to acknowledge Pala supremacy

Elephants could break formations and frighten horses, but disciplined archers, difficult ground, fire, or wounds could cause them to panic.

Pala campaigns often aimed to establish overlordship rather than directly annex every territory. A defeated ruler might remain in office after agreeing to pay tribute and provide troops.

### Siege and river warfare

Pala forces likely used:

- Elephants against gates
- Ladders
- Battering equipment
- Fire
- Mining
- Blockades
- Archery against walls

Bengal’s rivers made boats essential. Vessels transported soldiers, horses, elephants, supplies, and officials and could blockade river settlements.

The Pala fleet served defensive, commercial, and transportation roles rather than functioning only as a battle navy.

### Military quirks

The elephant corps was the army’s most intimidating component, but the empire’s network of subordinate rulers was equally important.

A Pala emperor could theoretically summon forces from across northern India. In practice, the army’s reliability depended on whether regional nobles remained loyal.

## Politics

The emperor ruled through royal officials, provincial authorities, landholding nobles, monasteries, and subordinate kings.

Pala rulers issued land grants to:

- Buddhist monasteries
- Hindu temples
- Brahmins
- Officials
- Military supporters

Government depended on control of agricultural revenue and cooperation from regional elites.

The empire’s political structure allowed rapid expansion under powerful rulers but could fragment under weaker ones. By 900, the Palas remained a major dynasty, though their ninth-century height had passed and several subordinate rulers were becoming increasingly independent.
