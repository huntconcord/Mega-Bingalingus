## Background
![[Pasted image 20260922123553.png]]

The First Bulgarian Empire was established in 681 after the Byzantine Empire recognized Bulgar control over territory south of the Danube River. Its population included the ruling Bulgars, numerous Slavic communities, and remnants of older Balkan populations.

Between 700 and 900, Bulgaria grew from a relatively new frontier state into one of the strongest powers in southeastern Europe. Its principal capitals during this period were Pliska and, beginning in 893, Preslav.

## Culture
![[Pasted image 20260922123621.png|231]]![[Pasted image 20260922123627.png|230]]![[Pasted image 20260922123746.png|227]]

Early Bulgarian society combined Bulgar and Slavic traditions. The Bulgars originally had a steppe-based culture and followed a religion often associated with the god Tangra, while the Slavs followed various polytheistic traditions.

Boris I converted Bulgaria to Christianity around 864–865. Christianity helped unify the empire’s different populations and connected Bulgaria more closely to Christian Europe.

Beginning in 886, students of Cyril and Methodius established religious and literary centers at Preslav and Ohrid. They translated Christian writings into Old Church Slavonic and trained Slavic-speaking clergy. Their work helped make Bulgaria a major center of Slavic literacy and laid the foundation for the Cyrillic alphabet.

## Military

### Organization
![[Pasted image 20260922124924.png|539]]

The Bulgarian army combined the traditions of the steppe-dwelling Bulgars with those of the empire’s Slavic population.

- Bulgar nobles and their followers formed the core of the cavalry.
- Slavic communities generally supplied infantry, archers, and light troops.
- Powerful nobles maintained their own armed retainers.
- Additional soldiers were raised from local communities during major wars.
- The khan personally commanded the army or appointed a trusted noble to lead it.

Bulgarian armies were usually smaller than Byzantine armies, but they compensated with mobility, surprise, and careful use of terrain.

### Gear and equipment
![[Pasted image 20260922125043.png|253]]![[Pasted image 20260922125126.png]]
Bulgar cavalrymen commonly carried:

- Composite bows for mounted archery
- Spears or lances
- Straight swords or curved sabres
- Daggers
- Round or oval shields

Wealthier warriors might wear mail, scale, or lamellar armor with a conical helmet. Poorer cavalrymen wore lighter protection so they could move quickly.

Slavic infantry commonly carried:

- Spears
- Javelins
- Bows
- Axes
- Swords or large knives
- Wooden shields

Armor was less common among ordinary infantry, although experienced or wealthy soldiers might possess helmets or mail.

### Tactics and fighting style

Bulgarian armies were strongly influenced by steppe warfare. Cavalry could attack from a distance with bows before closing with lances or swords.

Common tactics included:

- Feigned retreats intended to draw enemies out of formation
- Sudden cavalry attacks against exposed flanks
- Encirclement of slower enemy armies
- Ambushes in forests and mountain passes
- Night attacks and surprise assaults
- Cutting off an enemy’s supplies or retreat route
- Avoiding open battle until favorable conditions appeared

Slavic infantry was useful in forests, hills, and narrow terrain where cavalry had less room to operate.

The Bulgarians were especially skilled at using the Balkan landscape. They blocked mountain passes with wooden walls, trenches, and other obstacles before trapping enemy armies. This strategy contributed to the destruction of Emperor Nikephoros I’s army in 811.

### Siege warfare
![[Pasted image 20260922125232.png]]

Early Bulgarian armies were better suited to cavalry warfare than prolonged sieges. Under Khan Krum, however, Bulgaria captured Byzantine engineers and siege equipment.

Bulgarian forces began using:

- Battering rams
- Siege towers
- Stone-throwing machines
- Ladders
- Wooden defensive barriers

They also learned siege techniques from captured Byzantine soldiers, craftsmen, and cities.

### Military quirks

The Bulgarian army was known for patience. Commanders often allowed an invading army to advance before attacking it while it was tired, undersupplied, or retreating.

Bulgarian victories could also carry symbolic meaning. According to Byzantine chroniclers, Krum had the skull of Emperor Nikephoros lined with silver and used it as a drinking cup after the Byzantine defeat in 811.

## Politics
![[Pasted image 20260922125315.png|265]]

Bulgaria was ruled by khans until the Christianization of the empire changed its political traditions. The early government depended heavily on powerful Bulgar noble families.

During the mid-700s, struggles between noble factions caused frequent changes of rulers. Khan Krum later strengthened the central government and is traditionally associated with the creation of a written legal system.

Khan Omurtag signed a 30-year peace treaty with Byzantium in 815. He reorganized the administration and sponsored major construction projects.

Boris I used Christianity to strengthen royal authority and reduce the independence of the pagan nobility. In 870, the Bulgarian Church received its own archbishop and considerable autonomy.

In 893, Simeon I became ruler, the capital moved to Preslav, and Slavonic became the primary language of the government and church.
