## Background

The Wari developed around their capital near modern Ayacucho in Peru. They created one of the first large imperial systems in the Andes.

Wari influence extended across much of modern Peru through administrative centers, roads, colonization, religious practices, trade, and military pressure.

The Wari existed in 900 but had begun declining. Drought, internal conflict, weakened administration, and regional resistance may all have contributed to fragmentation.

## Culture

Wari culture combined influences from earlier highland traditions, the Nazca region, and the contemporary Tiwanaku civilization.

Wari religion prominently featured a figure often called the Staff God, along with:

- Winged beings
- Felines
- Serpents
- Birds
- Trophy heads
- Geometric supernatural imagery

Wari artisans produced exceptionally sophisticated:

- Textiles
- Ceramics
- Featherwork
- Metal objects
- Four-cornered hats

Elite tunics displayed complicated geometric patterns and probably communicated rank, office, ethnicity, or political authority.

Ceremonial drinking of maize beer, or _chicha_, helped form relationships between rulers, officials, and local communities.

## Military

### Organization

No written Wari military records survive. Archaeological evidence suggests forces drawn from:

- Elite warrior groups
- Regional levies
- Administrative centers
- Local allies
- Colonized populations
- Garrison troops

Military authority was probably connected to administrative officials who controlled fortified compounds, roads, food stores, and labor.

### Gear and equipment

Wari warriors likely used:

- Slings
- Spears
- Spear-throwers
- Clubs
- Stone or metal-headed maces
- Axes
- Knives
- Shields
- Bows in some regions

Slings were particularly effective in Andean warfare. An experienced slinger could strike from a considerable distance using stones readily available in mountainous terrain.

Elite warriors probably wore decorated tunics and distinctive headgear. Thick textiles may have provided limited protection, but the exact appearance of Wari combat armor remains uncertain.

### Tactics and fighting style

Likely tactics included:

- Sling volleys from elevated positions
- Ambushes in mountain passes
- Spear and club attacks at close range
- Securing valleys and water sources
- Rapid movement along imperial roads
- Establishing garrisons near subject populations
- Relocating cooperative or rebellious communities
- Combining military force with diplomacy and ceremonial exchange

Wari roads connected administrative centers, farms, storehouses, and defensible positions. These routes helped move armies and supplies through difficult terrain.

### Siege and defensive warfare

Wari administrative centers often possessed high walls, restricted entrances, and compartmentalized interiors.

Attacks against defended settlements may have used:

- Sling bombardment
- Fire
- Assaults against gates
- Blockades
- Cutting water supplies
- Occupying surrounding farmland

There is no evidence for large mechanical siege engines.

### Military quirks

The Wari did not rule through violence alone. They combined warfare with religious ceremony, gift exchange, feasting, resettlement, infrastructure, and cooperation with local elites.

Trophy-head imagery and archaeological evidence of violent treatment of captives nevertheless indicate that conquest could be extremely brutal.

## Politics

The Wari established planned administrative centers throughout their territory. These contained:

- Government compounds
- Storehouses
- Residential areas
- Ceremonial spaces
- Workshops
- Restricted courtyards

Officials used roads and storage facilities to collect and redistribute food, textiles, labor, and other resources.

Wari control varied by region. Some communities were closely administered, while others were connected through alliances, trade, shared religious practices, or indirect rule.

By 900, the empire remained culturally influential but was entering its final period of political fragmentation.
