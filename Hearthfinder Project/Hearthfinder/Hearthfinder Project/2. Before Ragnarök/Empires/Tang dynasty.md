## Background

The Tang dynasty reunified China after the collapse of the Sui dynasty. At its height, it exercised influence from Korea and northern Vietnam to Central Asia.

The An Lushan Rebellion of 755–763 severely weakened central authority. Provincial military governors called _jiedushi_ retained armies and revenue that the imperial government struggled to control.

During the late 800s, tax burdens, court factionalism, regional warlords, and the Huang Chao Rebellion devastated much of the empire.

The Tang dynasty still existed in 900, but only barely. Zhu Wen controlled the imperial court and ended the dynasty in 907.

## Culture

Tang China was one of the most culturally diverse states of its age.

Chang’an and other cities contained:

- Chinese communities
- Central Asian merchants
- Persians
- Sogdians
- Koreans
- Japanese visitors
- Buddhists
- Daoists
- Muslims
- Christians
- Zoroastrians

Poetry, painting, ceramics, music, metalworking, printing, and scholarship flourished.

Buddhism was highly influential, although Emperor Wuzong’s government suppressed Buddhist institutions between 843 and 845. Confucian education and the civil-service examination system remained important to government.

By 900, Chang’an had lost some of its former splendor because of rebellion and warfare, but Tang cultural traditions continued shaping China and neighboring societies long after the dynasty fell.

## Military

### Organization

Early Tang armies relied heavily on the _fubing_ militia system, but this had largely disappeared before 900.

Late Tang forces included:

- The imperial Shence Armies
- Professional provincial soldiers
- Armies loyal to _jiedushi_
- Palace guards
- Cavalry
- Infantry
- Crossbow units
- Local militias
- Tribal auxiliaries
- Foreign mercenaries

The Shence Armies protected the capital and came under the influence of palace eunuchs. Provincial commanders often treated their armies as personal forces.

### Gear and equipment

Tang soldiers used:

- Composite bows
- Crossbows
- Long spears
- Lances
- Straight swords
- Curved sabres
- Polearms
- Maces
- Shields

Armor included:

- Lamellar armor
- Scale armor
- Mail
- Armored coats
- Iron helmets
- Horse armor for some heavy cavalry

The state maintained military workshops and records of weapons and armor. In the early Tang, militia soldiers supplied much of their own equipment; professional forces increasingly received state-issued equipment.

### Tactics and fighting style

Tang armies had inherited a sophisticated combined-arms tradition.

Common tactics included:

- Crossbow volleys
- Massed archery
- Spear formations
- Heavy cavalry charges
- Mounted archery
- Cavalry attacks on the flanks
- Feigned retreats
- Defensive wagon or camp formations
- Keeping reserves behind the main line
- Coordinating separate infantry and cavalry commands

Tang commanders had learned extensively from Central Asian warfare. Cavalry remained especially important in the north and northwest.

By 900, however, there was no completely unified “Tang army.” Each warlord’s forces differed in quality, loyalty, and equipment.

### Siege warfare

Tang and regional Chinese armies used:

- Traction trebuchets
- Battering rams
- Siege towers
- Ladders
- Mining
- Flooding
- Fire attacks
- Mobile shields
- Blockades

Chinese cities were often protected by enormous rammed-earth walls. Sieges could therefore last months and cause severe starvation.

### Military quirks

The late Tang army’s main weakness was political loyalty. A commander might formally serve the emperor while fighting rival governors, withholding taxes, or negotiating independently.

Soldiers could remove commanders who failed to pay or reward them. Military units increasingly became attached to their provincial leaders rather than to the Tang state.

## Politics

Tang government combined the emperor, palace institutions, ministries, civil officials, military governors, and examination-trained administrators.

After the An Lushan Rebellion, many provinces became effectively hereditary military territories. The court also suffered from conflict between:

- Eunuch commanders
- Civil officials
- Aristocratic families
- Examination graduates
- Provincial warlords

By 900, the emperor remained symbolically important, but regional commanders possessed the real military power. The dynasty’s fall in 907 began the Five Dynasties and Ten Kingdoms period.
