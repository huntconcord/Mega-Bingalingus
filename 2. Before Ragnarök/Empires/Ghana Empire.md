## Background

The Ghana Empire, also known as Wagadu, developed in the Sahel across parts of modern Mauritania and Mali. It was not located in the modern country of Ghana.

The empire’s rulers belonged to the Soninke cultural world. Its location allowed it to benefit from growing trans-Saharan trade between North Africa and the gold-producing regions farther south.

Merchants brought salt, cloth, metal goods, horses, and luxury objects across the Sahara. Gold, ivory, leather, agricultural products, and enslaved people moved northward.

Written information about Ghana becomes much more detailed after 900. Consequently, descriptions of its ninth-century institutions must be reconstructed from archaeology and later accounts. Ancient Ghana had nevertheless emerged as a significant state-level society well before this date.

## Culture

Ghana’s population was predominantly Soninke and followed traditional West African religious practices.

Religious life probably involved:

- Ancestor veneration
- Sacred natural locations
- Ritual specialists
- Royal ceremonies
- Beliefs connecting political authority with spiritual power

Islam entered the region through North African and Saharan merchants. By 900, Muslim communities probably existed around important commercial settlements, but the king and most of the population remained followers of indigenous religions.

Soninke society included farmers, herders, merchants, craftspeople, nobles, warriors, and dependent communities. Oral historians preserved genealogies, traditions, agreements, and accounts of rulers.

Ironworking was essential to agriculture and warfare. Smiths produced tools, spearheads, arrowheads, knives, and other equipment while also occupying an important cultural position.

## Military

### Organization

The ninth-century Ghanaian army is poorly documented. It was probably assembled from:

- The king’s household warriors
- Retinues of regional chiefs
- Infantry levies
- Archers
- Spearmen
- Limited cavalry
- Troops provided by tributary communities

The army protected commercial routes, collected tribute, defended agricultural settlements, and enforced the king’s authority over subordinate rulers.

Most soldiers were probably infantry. Horses were valuable and difficult to maintain farther south because of climate and disease, although rulers could acquire them through Saharan trade.

### Gear and equipment

Ghanaian soldiers likely carried:

- Spears
- Javelins
- Bows and arrows
- Iron swords or large knives
- Axes
- Wooden or hide-covered shields

Ordinary troops probably fought without metal body armor. Thick cloth, leather, and shields offered more practical protection.

Elite warriors may have possessed:

- Imported horses
- Iron helmets
- Mail or other imported armor
- Higher-quality swords
- Decorated shields and clothing

Detailed descriptions of enormous Ghanaian armies come from later sources and should not be applied directly to the empire in 900.

### Tactics and fighting style

Ghana’s forces were well suited to the open savanna and Sahel.

Likely tactics included:

- Missile attacks by archers and javelin throwers
- Spearmen holding the main line
- Ambushes near wells and river crossings
- Raiding livestock and settlements
- Blocking trade routes
- Rapid attacks by elite cavalry
- Surrounding or intimidating rebellious communities
- Forcing opponents to resume tribute payments

Knowledge of wells, grazing areas, seasonal rivers, and desert-edge routes was strategically vital. An army that lost access to water could collapse without fighting a major battle.

### Fortifications and siege warfare

Important settlements could be protected by:

- Mud-brick or packed-earth walls
- Ditches
- Wooden barriers
- Defended entrances
- Elevated compounds

Siege warfare probably relied more on blockade, fire, assault, and starvation than on complicated mechanical siege engines.

### Military quirks

Control of trade was as important as battlefield victory. The king could punish an opponent by restricting access to salt, gold markets, wells, or caravan routes.

Ghana’s military strength therefore rested on both armed force and economic geography. A rival that could not reach markets or obtain necessary goods might be forced to submit.

## Politics

The ruler bore the title “Ghana,” usually interpreted as a royal or military title rather than the original name of the country.

The empire was probably a layered political system in which:

- The king directly controlled the central territory.
- Regional chiefs governed local communities.
- Subordinate rulers paid tribute.
- Merchants paid duties on valuable goods.
- Royal agents supervised major routes and markets.

The ruler’s wealth came from control and taxation of trade rather than ownership of every gold-producing area.

By 900, Ghana was an established regional power, although the extremely wealthy kingdom described by eleventh-century writers represents a somewhat later stage of its development.
