## Background

Around 700, the Frankish kingdoms were officially ruled by the Merovingian dynasty, but real authority increasingly belonged to the mayors of the palace.

Charles Martel unified much of the Frankish realm and defeated a Muslim raiding force at the Battle of Tours or Poitiers in 732. His son, Pepin the Short, removed the final Merovingian king and established the Carolingian dynasty in 751.

Pepin’s son Charlemagne ruled from 768 to 814. He conquered Saxony, Lombardy, Bavaria, and the Avar territories while expanding into northern Spain. Pope Leo III crowned him emperor in 800.

After Charlemagne’s death, Louis the Pious inherited the empire. Conflicts among Louis’s sons weakened the empire and resulted in its division through the Treaty of Verdun in 843.

## Culture

Frankish culture combined Germanic customs, Roman traditions, and Catholic Christianity. Latin remained the language of government, education, and the church, while ordinary people spoke numerous Germanic and Romance dialects.

Charlemagne sponsored the Carolingian Renaissance, which encouraged:

- The establishment of monastery and cathedral schools
- The education of clergy and government officials
- The copying of Roman and Christian writings
- The standardization of church services
- The development of Carolingian minuscule
- The preservation of Latin scholarship

Religion was closely tied to royal authority. Frankish rulers presented themselves as defenders of Christianity, while bishops and abbots frequently served as royal officials.

Charlemagne also used military force to convert conquered peoples. His Saxon campaigns included the destruction of pagan religious sites, forced baptisms, and severe punishments for abandoning Christianity.

## Military

### Organization

The Frankish army included:

- The ruler’s personal household troops
- Noble military retinues
- Free landowners required to serve
- Infantry levies
- Mounted elite warriors
- Troops supplied by counts, bishops, and monasteries
- Allied soldiers from conquered territories

Frankish rulers announced major campaigns at annual assemblies. Soldiers were expected to arrive with their own weapons, food, clothing, horses, and equipment.

The army was not yet a force of fully developed medieval knights. However, wealthy mounted warriors were becoming increasingly important.

### Gear and equipment

Wealthy Frankish warriors commonly carried:

- Spears or lances
- Straight, double-edged swords
- Seaxes or large fighting knives
- Javelins
- Round or oval shields
- Bows
- Daggers

Elite warriors might wear mail shirts and conical iron helmets. Metal armor was expensive and usually limited to nobles and professional retainers.

Ordinary infantry commonly used:

- Spears
- Axes
- Javelins
- Bows
- Knives
- Wooden shields

### Tactics and fighting style

Frankish infantry fought in organized spear and shield formations. Mounted warriors attacked enemy flanks, pursued retreating troops, and responded quickly to changing battlefield conditions.

Common tactics included:

- Combined infantry and cavalry attacks
- Raiding enemy settlements and supplies
- Attacking from multiple directions
- Using rivers to move soldiers and equipment
- Establishing fortified camps
- Taking hostages from conquered peoples
- Conducting repeated yearly campaigns

Frankish cavalrymen generally thrust or threw their spears. The couched-lance charge associated with later medieval knights was not yet the standard fighting style.

Charlemagne frequently defeated enemies through persistence. Rather than depending on one decisive battle, he returned year after year until his opponents submitted.

### Siege warfare

The Franks used:

- Battering rams
- Siege ladders
- Stone-throwing machines
- Siege towers
- Blockades
- Mining operations

Their ability to conduct long campaigns depended on royal estates, monasteries, roads, bridges, and river transportation.

### Military quirks

Military service was connected to personal loyalty, social status, and landholding. Rulers rewarded followers with wealth, political offices, and control of land.

The army was also closely associated with Christianity. Clergy could accompany campaigns, monasteries supplied troops and provisions, and wars were sometimes presented as efforts to defend or spread the Christian faith.

## Politics

The ruler governed through regional officials called counts. Counts administered justice, collected revenue, maintained order, and raised soldiers.

Charlemagne used officials called _missi dominici_ to inspect regional governments and investigate corruption. They usually traveled in pairs, with one church official and one secular noble.

The empire had no single permanent administrative capital. The royal court traveled between palaces, consuming supplies produced by royal estates.

Frankish political authority depended heavily on:

- Personal loyalty to the ruler
- Oaths between rulers and nobles
- Control of land
- Cooperation with the church
- Military success
- Distribution of wealth and offices

The Frankish tradition of dividing inheritance among sons created repeated succession disputes. These divisions eventually ended the unified empire in 843.
