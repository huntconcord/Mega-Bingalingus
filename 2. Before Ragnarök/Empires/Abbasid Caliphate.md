## Background

The Abbasid Caliphate was founded in 750 after a revolution overthrew the Umayyad Caliphate. The Abbasids claimed descent from Abbas, an uncle of the Prophet Muhammad.

The revolution received support from non-Arab Muslims, Persians, Shi’a groups, and others dissatisfied with Umayyad rule. The first Abbasid caliph was Abu al-Abbas al-Saffah.

Caliph al-Mansur founded Baghdad in 762. The city’s position near important land and river routes helped make it one of the medieval world’s greatest centers of trade, government, and scholarship.

## Culture
![[AC Clothing Art.png]]![[AC Traditional Woman's Clothing.png]]
Abbasid culture combined Arab, Persian, Greek, Indian, Central Asian, Christian, Jewish, and other intellectual traditions. Arabic became the leading language of government, literature, religion, and scholarship.

Baghdad became the center of a major translation movement. Scholars translated works on Greek philosophy, Persian government, and Indian mathematics into Arabic.

The Abbasid period produced important developments in:

- Algebra and mathematics
- Astronomy
- Medicine
- Geography
- Philosophy
- Engineering
- Literature and history

The caliphate also supported a vast commercial network connecting Europe, Africa, the Middle East, India, Central Asia, and China. Cities such as Baghdad, Basra, Kufa, and Samarra became important cultural and economic centers.
## Military

![[AC 9th Cent Soldier.png]]

### Organization

The Abbasid military was extremely diverse because the caliphate ruled territory stretching from North Africa to Central Asia.

Its armies included:

- Arab tribal troops
- Persian and Khurasani soldiers
- Professional cavalry
- Infantry archers and spearmen
- Frontier volunteers
- Provincial forces supplied by governors
- Turkish slave-soldiers known as ghilman

The early Abbasids relied heavily on Khurasani soldiers who had supported their revolution. During the 800s, the caliphs increasingly recruited Turkish soldiers from Central Asia.

These Turkish troops were trained as professional mounted warriors and were personally dependent on the caliph. However, their commanders eventually gained substantial political power.

![[AC Calvary.png]]
### Gear and equipment

Abbasid cavalry commonly carried:

- Composite bows
- Spears or lances
- Straight or slightly curved swords
- Maces
- Daggers
- Round shields

Wealthier cavalrymen wore mail, scale, or lamellar armor. Helmets were generally conical and could include mail protection for the neck and face.

Infantry commonly used:

- Spears
- Swords
- Bows
- Javelins
- Large or medium-sized shields

Armor depended on a soldier’s wealth and position. Professional guards and experienced soldiers were better equipped than temporary provincial troops.

### Tactics and fighting style

Abbasid armies used combined-arms tactics. Infantry held the main line while archers weakened the enemy and cavalry attacked the flanks.

Common tactics included:

- Mounted archery
- Cavalry flanking attacks
- Repeated attacks followed by organized withdrawals
- Infantry spear lines protected by shields
- Missile attacks before close combat
- Holding cavalry in reserve until the enemy became disorganized
- Raiding enemy territory rather than immediately attempting conquest

Against the Byzantines, Abbasid armies frequently conducted seasonal raids into Anatolia. These raids damaged fortifications, captured prisoners, collected loot, and prevented Byzantium from becoming too strong along the frontier.

The caliphate’s enormous size meant its armies often fought differently depending on the region. Forces in Central Asia emphasized mounted warfare, while armies in Iraq and Syria relied more heavily on mixed infantry and cavalry.

### Siege warfare
![[AC Mangonel.png]]
Abbasid armies possessed sophisticated siege equipment and engineers. They used:

- Stone-throwing mangonels
- Battering rams
- Siege towers
- Mining beneath defensive walls
- Fire arrows and incendiary weapons
- Large protective shields for advancing soldiers

Special troops known as naffatun carried incendiary substances, including naphtha. They threw containers of burning material or used it against fortifications and enemy soldiers.

### Military quirks

The Abbasid army’s greatest strength was its diversity. The caliphs could recruit soldiers familiar with desert warfare, mountain combat, siege warfare, and the mounted traditions of Central Asia.

However, this diversity also caused rivalries between Arab, Persian, Turkish, and provincial troops. By the late 800s, caliphs sometimes had less control over their own soldiers than the soldiers’ commanders did.

## Politics

![[AC Polotics.png]]

The Abbasid caliphs ruled through an organized bureaucracy influenced by Persian government traditions. Viziers supervised administration, while provincial governors collected taxes and maintained order.

The caliphate reached a political and economic high point under Harun al-Rashid, who ruled from 786 to 809. After his death, his sons al-Amin and al-Ma’mun fought a destructive civil war.

Al-Ma’mun won the war and supported scholarship, but he also introduced the Mihna, a religious test designed to enforce his theological position. Resistance from religious scholars weakened the caliph’s claim to control religious doctrine.

In 836, the capital moved from Baghdad to Samarra. After 861, Turkish commanders increasingly controlled the government.

By 900, provincial dynasties such as the Aghlabids, Tulunids, Tahirids, and Saffarids ruled with considerable independence. The Abbasid caliph remained an important religious and symbolic leader, but his direct political control had declined.
