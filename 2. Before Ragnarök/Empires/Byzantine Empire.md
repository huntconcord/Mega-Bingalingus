## Background

The Byzantine Empire was the eastern continuation of the Roman Empire. Its people called themselves Romans, although Greek had become the empire’s principal language.

By 700, Byzantium had lost Syria, Egypt, Palestine, and North Africa to Muslim conquest. It retained Constantinople, Anatolia, portions of the Balkans and Greece, several islands, and territories in southern Italy.

Despite invasions and internal conflict, Constantinople remained one of the largest, wealthiest, and best-defended cities in the medieval world.

## Culture
![[BE Royalty.png|440]]![[BE Garb.png|260]]

Byzantine culture combined Roman government and law with Greek language, education, and philosophy. Christianity was central to Byzantine identity, government, and everyday life.

The Iconoclast Controversy divided the empire during the 700s and early 800s. Iconoclasts opposed the religious use of sacred images, while icon supporters defended their veneration.

The use of icons was permanently restored under Empress Theodora in 843. This event became known as the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

Byzantine missionaries also spread Christianity and literacy among Slavic peoples. Cyril and Methodius created the Glagolitic alphabet and translated Christian writings into a Slavic language. Their students later continued their work in Bulgaria.

Under the Macedonian dynasty, beginning in 867, the empire entered a period of renewed interest in classical literature, law, education, and art.

## Military

### Organization
![[BE Calvary.png]]
The Byzantine military combined regional armies with professional imperial troops.

The two most important categories were:

- **Theme soldiers:** Provincial forces responsible for defending particular regions.
- **Tagmata:** Professional imperial regiments stationed near Constantinople and commanded by officers trusted by the emperor.

The thematic armies defended their homes and responded to raids. The tagmata formed the empire’s central field army and accompanied the emperor on major campaigns.

The Byzantine military also included foreign mercenaries, allied troops, scouts, engineers, and specialized guards.

### Gear and equipment

![[BE Soldiers.png]]

Byzantine cavalry commonly carried:

- A long spear or lance
- A sword called a spathion
- A composite bow
- Javelins
- Round shields

Heavily equipped cavalrymen wore mail or lamellar armor called a klibanion. Some also used armored protection for their arms, legs, or horses. Lighter cavalry depended more heavily on bows, speed, and scouting.

Byzantine infantry commonly carried:

- Long spears
- Swords
- Bows
- Javelins
- Large shields

Heavy infantry formed defensive lines with shields and spears. Archers and lighter troops attacked from behind or between these formations.

### Tactics and fighting style

![[BE Tactics.png]]

The Byzantines preferred careful, organized warfare over aggressive frontal attacks. Commanders were expected to study the enemy, scout the terrain, and preserve their soldiers whenever possible.

Common tactics included:

- Using infantry to hold the center
- Placing cavalry on the wings
- Keeping reserves behind the main army
- Drawing enemies into ambushes
- Pretending to retreat
- Attacking enemy supply lines
- Blocking mountain passes
- Avoiding battle when conditions were unfavorable
- Using diplomacy, bribes, or alliances to divide enemies

Against Arab raiders, Byzantine forces often followed the invading army instead of immediately confronting it. They waited until the raiders were burdened with loot or attempting to cross a mountain pass before attacking.

Against steppe cavalry and Bulgarians, Byzantine commanders attempted to protect their infantry formations and prevent enemy horsemen from surrounding them.

### Defensive and siege warfare

The Byzantines maintained fortified cities, frontier strongholds, signal stations, and defensive walls throughout the empire. Constantinople’s walls were among the strongest fortifications in the world.

Byzantine engineers used:

- Stone-throwing artillery
- Battering rams
- Siege towers
- Tunnels and counter-tunnels
- Defensive fire weapons
- Portable barriers and fortified camps

The Byzantines often avoided risking their armies in open battle because fortified cities could delay an invasion until enemy supplies ran out.

### The Byzantine navy

![[BE Dromon.png]]

The navy was an essential part of Byzantine defense. Its principal warship was the dromon, a fast, oared vessel that could also use sails.

Byzantine ships carried:

- Archers
- Marines
- Ramming or boarding equipment
- Stone-throwing weapons
- Greek fire

Greek fire was projected through tubes or thrown in containers. It was especially frightening because it could continue burning on the surface of the water. It helped defend Constantinople during the Arab siege of 717–718.

### Military quirks

The Byzantine military placed unusual emphasis on organization, intelligence, and survival. Commanders used scouts, spies, signal systems, written reports, and detailed campaign plans.

Byzantine strategy did not always require destroying an enemy army. Bribing one enemy to attack another, arranging a marriage alliance, encouraging a rebellion, or waiting for an invading army to run out of supplies could be considered just as successful as winning a battle.

## Politics

![[BE Politics.png]]

The Byzantine emperor held enormous political, military, and religious influence. He was supported by a professional bureaucracy based in Constantinople and by provincial military governors.

The empire experienced frequent changes of rulers during the early 700s. Leo III restored stability after becoming emperor in 717 and founded the Isaurian dynasty.

Religious policy became closely connected to imperial politics during the Iconoclast Controversy. Several emperors attempted to restrict icons, bringing them into conflict with monks, religious leaders, and the papacy.

Relations with Western Europe weakened as Byzantine authority declined in Italy. In 800, Pope Leo III crowned Charlemagne “Emperor of the Romans,” challenging Byzantium’s claim to be the only Roman Empire.

Basil I seized the throne in 867 and founded the Macedonian dynasty. He strengthened the government, reorganized Byzantine law, and began a period of recovery. By 900, the empire was more stable and prepared for the military and cultural expansion of the following century.
